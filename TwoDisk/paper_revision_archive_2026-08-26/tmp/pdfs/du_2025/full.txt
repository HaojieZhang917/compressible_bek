This study targets the stability issues of rotating-disk boundary layer in a rotor-stator
cavity within the context of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, incorporating a
drag model representative of roughness elements. Consequently, 32 azimuthally uniformly
distributed roughness elements have been positioned within the computational domain.
Owing to the inviscid crossﬂow instability inherent to the rotating-disk boundary layer,
the roughness elements induce crossﬂow vortices with an azimuthal wave numberk = 32.
Upon saturation of the crossﬂow vortices, a transition to turbulence does not immediately
ensue; instead, a series of azimuthal Fourier modes constituting self-sustaining, spatiotem-
porally coherent traveling spiral waves mode emerge downstream. At higher Reynolds
numbers, these traveling spiral waves mode reach saturation and are followed downstream
by a transition to turbulence. Notably, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the ﬁrst
instance where selective excitation of crossﬂow vortices with a speciﬁc wave number in
the rotating-disk boundary layer within a rotor-stator cavity has resulted in a transitional
process ultimately dominated by traveling mode spiral waves. This process is distinct from
the one observed over a single rotating disk, where the crossﬂow vortices, after reaching a
certain Reynolds number, govern the transition to turbulence in the von Kármán boundary
layer.

I. INTRODUCTION
The rotor-stator cavity holds paramount importance in facilitating the seamless operation of
aviation turbine engines, prompting extensive scholarly focus on comprehending the intricate ﬂow
dynamics and heat transfer phenomena that manifest within it [ 1]. Typically, the ﬂow inside the
cavity exhibits a characteristic Batchelor ﬂow pattern, with well-deﬁned boundary layers present
on both the stationary and rotating walls, separated by a central rotating core. Due to the rotational
pumping effect, the ﬂow within the boundary layer on the rotor moves from the lower radius to the
higher radius, while on the stator, it moves in the opposite direction, from the higher radius to the
lower radius. The strong three-dimensional rotation engenders a wealth of complex ﬂow structures
within the boundary layers of both the rotor and stator [2,3]. Investigation of these boundary layers

not only enables a comprehensive understanding of the ﬂow and heat transfer mechanisms within
the cavity but also provides fresh insights into the transition process of rotating ﬂows [4].
The simpliﬁed model consists of a stationary disk and a rotating disk, each with a radius R∗,
separated vertically by a distance of 2H∗. Typically, a shroud is present at the top of the disk [5,6],
and a rotating hub is situated at the bottom [ 7–9]. When secondary ﬂows are introduced near the
lower radius of the disk either radially or axially, researchers are particularly concerned with the
aerodynamic drag or heat transfer on the disk surface, which are critical metrics for the performance
of the rotor-stator cavity in aviation engines [10,11]. Regardless of whether it concerns aerodynamic
drag or heat transfer, these factors are intimately connected to the ﬂow conditions on the surface of
the disk. Laminar and turbulent ﬂows critically inﬂuence these parameters. Therefore, it is essential
to ﬁrst elucidate the transition issues within the boundary layers of the rotor-stator cavity to obtain
more accurate engineering parameters such as aerodynamic drag and heat transfer.

can reach 10

rotating disks may exist in a state where laminar-transitional-turbulent ﬂow coexist.
Around this Reynolds number, numerous numerical, theoretical, and experimental studies have
investigated the characteristics of boundary layer ﬂow within rotor-stator cavity [ 6,16–22]. For
the stationary-disk boundary layer, the transition from laminar instability to turbulence occurs
very rapidly, and most studies focus only on its laminar instability. The typical characteris-
tics of instability include large-scale spiral waves distributed azimuthally and axisymmetric ring
waves. Through linear stability analysis, these two types of instabilities are deﬁned as Type I
inviscid crossﬂow instability, caused by a radial velocity inﬂection point, and Type II viscous
instability, resulting from the interaction between Coriolis forces and viscous forces. The critical
Reynolds number for Type I instability is higher than that for Type II instability, leading these
instabilities to occur at higher and lower radii of the boundary layer, respectively. However,
according to research by Cros et al. [23] it is highly probable that the nonlinear interaction
between these two types of instabilities leads to the transition of the stationary-disk boundary
layer to turbulence. Recently, Xie et al. [24] have further emphasized this phenomenon through
direct numerical simulation and global linear stability analysis, highlighting that the nonlinear
interaction between inviscid crossﬂow instability and viscous instability facilitates the emergence
of localized turbulence in the stationary-disk boundary layer. They also noted that the distur-
bances from vigorous turbulence in the stationary-disk boundary layer could propagate across
the core region and impact the rotating-disk boundary layer due to axial migration of the cavity
ﬂow. Therefore, to fully understand the destabilization mechanisms of the rotating-disk boundary
layer, the inﬂuence of vigorous turbulence in the stationary-disk boundary layer must not be
overlooked, as it may affect the linear instability processes within the stationary disk’s boundary
layer.
For the rotating-disk boundary layer, its ﬂow and instability characteristics are similar to those
of the stationary disk, but transition to turbulent occurs at higher Reynolds numbers. According
to research by Yim et al. and Séverac et al. [6,12]a tR e = 4 × 10
5, the boundary layer can be
divided into laminar, transitional, and turbulent regions, with the transition to turbulence being
particularly pronounced. Similar to the case with a single rotating disk, the spiral waves transi-
tion to turbulence through convective or absolute instability. During convective instability, spiral
waves emerge from a lower radius, and typically, their azimuthal wave number is not large. In
the research conducted by Séverac et al. and Xie et al. [12,21] there are about a dozen spiral
waves. However, due to the merging and transformation of spiral waves at higher radii, the exact

number is difﬁcult to discern. The spiral waves begin to develop from a lower radius and, at

In the rotor-stator cavity, the absolute instability process of the rotating-disk boundary layer
was discovered by Yim et al. [6] through numerical simulations. When no convective instability
spiral waves appear at lower radii, rapidly growing spiral waves occur near the critical Reynolds
number for absolute instability. Those spiral waves corresponding several elephant front-forming
global modes, form a steep front, eventually leading to incipient turbulence. Those spiral waves
corresponding to absolute instability have a relatively high azimuthal wave number, typically
between 60 and 80. It is important to emphasize that this type of process can only occur on smooth
disks. For realistic surfaces with unavoidable roughness elements on rotating disks, disturbances
generated by roughness elements trigger the spiral waves of convective instability. These spiral
waves appear at lower radii and are relatively stationary to the disk, at which point they are also
known as crossﬂow vortices (stationary mode). In contrast, the traveling spiral waves mode implies
that the spiral waves have relative motion with the rotating disk. In Yim et al. ’s [6] research,
the spiral waves of absolute instability belong to the traveling spiral waves mode. Unfortunately,
Séverac et al. [12] did not specify the relative motion state of the spiral waves in relation to the disk
in their results. However, theoretical analysis suggests that convectively instability spiral waves can
be either stationary mode or traveling spiral waves mode, while spiral waves of absolute instability
are deﬁnitively traveling spiral waves mode.
Recent research on a single rotating disk has identiﬁed global instability, which features an
azimuthal wave number even greater than that observed for absolute instability [25]. However, under
the rapid growth of convective instability, which triggers nonlinear effects before the disturbances
corresponding to global instability can develop sufﬁciently across the radial extent, no transition
processes dominated by global instability have been observed in experiments or simulations. This
suggests that while global instability may be theoretically interesting, its practical impact on the
ﬂow transition in typical experimental or simulations might be limited due to the dominance of
convective processes.
Although existing research following the work on the von Kármán boundary layer has prelimi-
narily indicated potential instability processes in rotor-stator cavity, there are still some signiﬁcant
additional factors that have not been explicitly discussed. For instance, when the rotating-disk
boundary layer is transitioning to turbulence, could vigorous turbulence on the stationary-disk
boundary layer introduce additional disturbances through the core area? To what extent will the
strong disturbances introduced by the hub decay or grow? Most importantly, for the turbine disk
cavities in aeroengine, what kind of transition processes might the surface roughness elements
induce?
Study into these questions could elucidate the similarities and differences between the rotating-
disk boundary layer in rotor-stator cavity and the von Kármán boundary layer on a single inﬁnite
rotating disk. This could enable better application of the theoretical work on the von Kármán
boundary layer to the disk boundary layers in rotor-stator cavity. The current study focuses on
the convective instability processes induced by crossﬂow vortices, which arise due to roughness
elements. For the von Kármán boundary layer, Imayama et al. [26] conducted an experimental
study using 32 uniformly distributed roughness elements to investigate the convective instability and
destabilization process of the von Kármán boundary layer. In simulations, directly implementing
wall protrusions can be challenging. Therefore, we opted to represent roughness elements using
drag source terms proportional to the relative velocity [ 27] and employed spectral vanishing
viscosity (SVV) for direct numerical simulation to stabilize the ﬂow. It is important to note
that due to model-induced discrepancies, even with the same number of roughness elements,
our current simulation results differ from Imayama et al. ’s [ 26] experimental outcomes. Con-
sequently, we have not compared our results with those of Imayama et al. ’s [26] study in our
presentation.

B. Spectral vanishing viscosity method
To mitigate the need for high spatial resolution of the computational grid, the SVV method is
implemented to stabilize the ﬂow. Speciﬁcally, when the wave number exceeds a speciﬁed threshold,
controlled artiﬁcial dissipation is introduced in the diffusion terms of the momentum equations.
This is necessary because inadequate spatial resolution leads to energy accumulation at high wave
numbers, resulting in numerical oscillations that compromise the stability and accuracy of the
calculations. By employing this method, viscosity is increased at high wave numbers, effectively
suppressing these oscillations and dissipating the accumulated energy. It has been demonstrated in
Ref. [29], within the context of spectral element formulations, that the use of SVV does not affect
the convergence properties of the method. References [30,31] offer a more elaborate exposition on
the SVV technique, encompassing an analysis of the effects of control parameters.

III. RESULTS
A. Crossﬂow vortices mode
At Re = 2 × 105,Y i met al. [6] observed that the majority of the rotating-disk boundary layer
remains in a laminar state, except near the shroud where a spiral waves structure with azimuthal
wave number k = 82 appears due to strong shear and centrifugal effects by the shroud. In Fig.4(a),
we present the axial velocity w contour plot of the rotating-disk boundary layer under the same
conditions, where w ranges from −0.01 (blue) to 0.01 (red). In all the contour plots characterizing
the boundary layer, the rotation direction of rotating disk is counterclockwise.
The results exhibit a similar behavior to Yim et al.’s [6] ﬁndings, with a uniform occurrence of
k = 75 spiral waves structures in the azimuthal direction. It is noteworthy that the axial velocity,
w, is much smaller than both the radial and azimuthal velocities, and approaches zero within the
boundary layer. Therefore, it serves as an excellent reference for disturbances in the rotating-disk
boundary layer. Consequently, in the contour plots presented in this study, the colors represent the
axial velocity w without any additional transformations.

phenomena that would arise from secondary instabilities of the spiral waves [38,39].
IV . CONCLUSION
Unlike the traditionally single rotating disk, detailed trasitional processes brought by roughness
elements, and hence, under the selective excitation of crossﬂow vortices, are still unknown until
now. This study employs a Fourier/spectral element-based spectral vanishing viscosity method to
conduct large eddy simulations (LES-SVV). A resistance source term based on the local relative
velocity squared is added to the momentum equation. The objective is to investigate the effects
of roughness elements on rotating-disk in a rotor-stator cavity on instability and furthermore, on
transition to turbulence. The main ﬁndings of this study can be summarized as follows:
(i) Through the analysis of eight simulation cases, the current grid resolution and numerical
methods have been initially validated to be capable of capturing the spiral waves structure with
an azimuthal wave number of 75 near the shroud due to the strong centrifugal and shear effects,
as observed in Yim et al. ’s [6] study. Additionally, at a lower Reynolds number Re = 2 × 10
5,
stationary mode spiral waves excited by 32 azimuthal uniform roughness elements have been
identiﬁed starting from r = 6, which almost entirely occupy the entire disk boundary layer. The
peak of v
rms occurs at an axial length ofzδ = 1.31. This value is remarkably close tozδ = 1.3, which
corresponds to a von Kármán boundary layer in single rotating disk. When the drag coefﬁcient
S = 100, there is an increase in the disturbance intensity of the crossﬂow vortices, which grow
slowly downstream with a modest growth rate, indicative of an increase in radial wavelength. At
higher radii, the crossﬂow vortices corresponding to drag coefﬁcients S = 10 and S = 100 both
remain unsaturated.
(ii) When the Reynolds number is Re = 3 × 10
5, regardless of whether S = 10 or S = 100, the
roughness elements-induced crossﬂow vortices reach saturation. Interestingly, despite the saturation

of the crossﬂow vortices and the apparent nonlinear effects, they do not destabilize to cause local
turbulence. Instead, traveling spiral waves mode emerge following the stationary mode spiral waves.
These traveling spiral waves modes are predominantly concentrated between Fourier modes 10 to
20 and dominate the disturbance at higher radial positions of the rotating-disk noundary layer in the
rotor-stator cavity. Furthermore, signiﬁcant ﬂuctuations in kinetic energy are observed for Fourier
modes with high azimuthal wave numbers ranging from 80 to 120, corresponding to the disturbances
near the shroud in case 1. Speciﬁcally, the disturbances excite traveling spiral waves mode, which
persist even when the stationary mode spiral waves and roughness elements are removed. As the
stationary mode spiral waves disappear at higher radii, the transition towards turbulence in the
current study is dominated by traveling spiral waves mode with azimuthal wave numbersk between
10 and 20. Due to the periodic and interconnected evolution of these traveling modes, which are
coherent structures in both time and space, there is no clear distinction in their azimuthal wave
numbers.
(iii) At a Reynolds number of Re= 4 × 10
5, the disturbance energy of the traveling spiral waves
mode reaches saturation around a radius of rδ = 462.3. However, it is not until approximately rδ =
524.0 that all the traveling spiral waves mode completely lose their characteristic features, resulting
in the onset of turbulence. The disparity in the radius where spiral waves instability occurs can be
attributed to the fact that, despite the coherence in time and space, the traveling spiral waves mode
consist of individual spiral waves with distinct characteristics, particularly manifested in the tilt
angle of the spiral waves.
This study has discussed the transition from laminar to turbulent ﬂow in the rotating-disk
boundary layer, induced by 32 uniformly distributed roughness elements. Considering the destabi-
lization mechanisms of the spiral waves themselves, the research parameter k = 32 is not a unique
azimuthal wave number. When spiral waves of other azimuthal wave numbers are excited, the
destabilization processes and conclusions observed in this study will also occur, indicating that
the current conclusions can be generalized. However, there are still some issues that remain to be
resolved. The current disturbances have induced self-sustaining traveling spiral waves mode, but
it is unclear why the azimuthal wave numbers of these traveling spiral waves modes are conﬁned
to the range of 10 to 20. It is highly probable that the initial development of the traveling spiral
waves mode corresponds to the modal azimuthal wave numbers of 10–20 at r
δ, which have the
highest growth rates, similar to the large-scale spiral waves observed on the rotating-disk boundary
layer of rotor-stator cavity with a rotating hub. Similarly, it is possible that the saturated stationary
mode spiral waves induced by the roughness elements have altered the distribution of the base ﬂow,
alter the number of spiral waves and preventing the onset of absolute instability on the rotating-disk
boundary layer. Providing accurate answers to these questions will require further investigation
using methodologies such as linear stability analysis to explore the characteristic parameters of
spiral waves with different azimuthal wave numbers in subsequent work.
Although the current SVV method is theoretically sound and does not signiﬁcantly affect the
transition process, it remains necessary in subsequent work to use a grid with sufﬁcient resolution
and to minimize the impact of artiﬁcial viscosity. This approach will facilitate the exploration of the
absolute and convective instability characteristics of the rotating-disk boundary layer in the fully
nonlinear Navier-Stokes equations.
